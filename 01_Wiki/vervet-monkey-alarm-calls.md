# Vervet Monkey Alarm Calls

Vervet monkeys (*Chlorocebus pygerythrus*) produce acoustically distinct alarm calls for different predator classes — aerial predators, large felids, and snakes — and conspecifics respond with behaviors appropriate to each predator type, not just to the call's alarm character. This is the founding study in referential animal signalling.

## What the Signal Is

Three acoustically distinct call types:
- **Raptor alarm** (short, tonal bark) — response: animals look up, run into bushes
- **Leopard alarm** (loud, staccato bark) — response: animals climb trees, scan the ground
- **Snake alarm** (high-pitched chutter) — response: animals stand bipedally, scan grass

The calls differ in acoustic structure. Crucially, receivers do not merely become "alarmed" — they execute predator-specific evasive behaviors even when they cannot see the predator themselves.

## What It Encodes

Specific predator-class identity, not just danger level. The call encodes something like a referent: "raptor overhead," "leopard on ground," "snake in grass." This is distinct from signals that encode emotional arousal.

Playback experiments (Seyfarth & Cheney 1980) confirmed this: broadcasting recorded alarm calls to animals who could not see any predator produced the predator-appropriate evasive response.

## Honest or Deceptive

Honest. Vervets do not use alarm calls to manipulate competitors — false alarms would erode receiver responsiveness and represent a true cost. The signal's value depends on its reliability.

Compare [[fork-tailed-drongo-alarm-calls]] (when it exists) for the deceptive variant: drongos produce false vervet-style alarms to steal food.

## Acquisition

Primarily innate for the basic call types, but refinement is learned. Young vervets produce alarm calls to non-predators (e.g., eagles for any bird overhead). Observational learning from adults sharpens discrimination over the first year of life. The call *type* is innate; the *referential accuracy* is partially learned.

## Key Researcher / Discovery Story

Robert Seyfarth and Dorothy Cheney conducted fieldwork in Amboseli, Kenya, publishing the foundational study in 1980 (*Science*, 210:801–803). The paper was significant not because it showed that animals have predator-specific calls (known before) but because the playback design demonstrated that the calls functioned as referential signals *for receivers* — the response was to the encoded predator class, not to social context or acoustic alarm character alone.

The study opened thirty years of debate: does referential signalling require intentionality? Is "referential" the same cognitive operation as human reference? Peter Marler called it "the most compelling evidence for semanticity in nonhuman communication."

## Open Questions

- Do vervets understand the *relationship* between call and referent, or do they have hardwired response-to-call mappings? (The "functional reference" vs. "mental representation" debate)
- Can vervets *combine* calls into sequences with compositional meaning? (Evidence is thin for vervets; stronger in Campbell's monkeys — see [[campbells-monkey-suffixation]])
- Cross-population variation in call structure is modest; is there any cultural drift comparable to [[orca-pod-calls]]?

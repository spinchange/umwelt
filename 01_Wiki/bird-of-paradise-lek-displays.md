# Birds-of-Paradise — Correlated Complexity in Lek Displays

The 44 species of birds-of-paradise (family Paradisaeidae) represent **runaway sexual selection operating across 24 million years**, producing the most elaborate multimodal display system documented in any non-human taxon. A 2018 *PLOS Biology* study (Ligon et al.) showed that the different signal channels — plumage color, plumage structure, dance choreography, and acoustic signals — have evolved in a correlated fashion: species with more complex plumage also have more complex dances and more complex vocalizations. The whole display system co-evolves as a unit, driven by female preference.

## What the Signals Are

Displays are multimodal, combining:
- **Elaborate feather plumes** — elongated tail wires, breast shields, flank plumes; structural color via iridescent barbules (not pigment); some species convert feather structure to create black-hole ultra-matte patches that absorb >99.95% of light
- **Choreographed dance** — species-specific, highly stereotyped movement sequences; some species (parotias) perform elaborate ballet-like routines including wing-spreading, head-shaking, and spin sequences
- **Sound** — wing modifications in some males produce mechanical sounds during display; vocal calls frame the display
- **Stage preparation** — some species clear court areas of debris, or use specific display perches oriented to maximize visual impact

At least 8 species use **lek mating systems** — communal display arenas where multiple males display simultaneously; females visit and choose. Competition between displaying males produces continual refinement of display quality.

## Female-Driven Evolution

The radical diversity of display forms within the family is attributed almost entirely to female mate choice:

- Males in polygynous species are **sexually dimorphic** with elaborate plumage; females are drab (camouflage during incubation)
- Males take up to **7 years** to develop full adult plumage — a long developmental period that may itself signal health and genetic quality (surviving long enough to reach full display capability)
- Young males in female-like plumage can occasionally mate opportunistically before reaching full display maturity, suggesting the system is not completely rigid
- Females show **variable preferences** across populations; male displays evolve to match local female preferences

The diversity of display forms across 44 species, despite relatively recent common ancestry, suggests that female preferences can drive rapid morphological divergence — different species went down different "runaway" trajectories.

## Correlated Complexity

The Ligon et al. (2018) study is the key finding: traits do not vary independently. Species with more plumage elements have more complex dances and more acoustic complexity. This means:
1. Female preference is selecting on the whole display package, not single traits
2. The signals are **informationally redundant** across modalities — a female has multiple channels to assess the same underlying genetic quality
3. Evolutionary elaboration in one channel tends to pull elaboration in others (genetic correlation or reinforcing selection)

This is the opposite of the "trade-off" hypothesis (which would predict that species investing in plumage should invest less in dance). The display system is a **coherent unit** shaped by unified female preference.

## Darwin's Connection

Charles Darwin discussed birds-of-paradise extensively in *The Descent of Man* (1871) as evidence for sexual selection — the idea that female choice could drive the evolution of traits with no survival value (and indeed some cost). The birds-of-paradise were his most compelling examples, alongside peacocks. Alfred Russel Wallace, who independently co-discovered natural selection, collected and described many species during his six years in the Malay Archipelago.

## Open Questions

- What is the cognitive mechanism by which females evaluate multimodal displays? Is it a gestalt assessment, or sequential evaluation of components?
- The ultra-matte black feathers (superblack patches) create extreme contrast that makes adjacent colored plumes appear more vivid — a cognitive manipulation of the receiver's visual processing. How widespread is this?
- Are display preferences culturally transmitted among females, analogous to [[orca-pod-dialects]]?

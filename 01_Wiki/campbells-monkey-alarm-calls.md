# Campbell's Mona Monkey — Predator-Specific Alarm Calls and Call Combinations

Campbell's mona monkeys (*Cercopithecus campbelli*) of West Africa produce a small set of discrete alarm calls that encode predator type and urgency — and, crucially, combine those calls using a suffix morpheme that modifies meaning. This is one of the most compelling cases for proto-syntax in animal communication: a non-human species demonstrably combining call units to produce meanings not present in the individual units.

## What the Signal Is

Males produce two primary alarm call types:

- **"Krak"** — a loud, sharp call given to leopards (terrestrial predators)
- **"Hok"** — a softer, rising call given to crowned eagles (aerial predators)

These calls elicit predator-appropriate responses in conspecifics: "krak" triggers ground-level vigilance and movement toward cover; "hok" triggers upward scanning and movement into lower vegetation.

But males also produce modified versions:

- **"Krak-oo"** — "krak" + "-oo" suffix: given to a wider range of disturbances, including falling branches and non-predator disturbances; receivers treat it as a general disturbance alert, not a leopard-specific call
- **"Hok-oo"** — "hok" + "-oo" suffix: given to general aerial disturbances, including non-eagle birds; receivers treat it as a general aerial alert

The "-oo" suffix systematically **broadens** the referent of the base call. This is the key finding: the same suffix applied to different base calls produces predictably modified meanings in both cases — a morphological rule, not arbitrary variation.

## What They Encode

The base calls encode specific predator identity. The suffixed calls encode a broader threat category. Together the system can express:

| Call | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Krak | Leopard specifically |
| Krak-oo | General ground-level disturbance |
| Hok | Crowned eagle specifically |
| Hok-oo | General aerial disturbance |
| Hok + Krak sequence | "Come look at this" — used when Diana monkey alarm calls are heard; invites investigation rather than escape |

That final combination — hok calls followed by krak calls — is produced specifically when males hear Diana monkey alarm calls (from a neighboring species), and it appears to function as a "recruit-to-investigate" signal rather than a pure alarm. It is not reducible to either base call's meaning.

## Honest or Deceptive

Honest. Unlike the [[fork-tailed-drongo-false-alarms|fork-tailed drongo]] that exploits alarm call comprehension for kleptoparasitism, Campbell's monkey calls appear to accurately reflect the nature of the perceived threat. The broadened "-oo" calls are not false alarms — they're used when genuine but less specific threats occur.

## Acquisition

Innate structural framework, with evidence of social refinement. Young males produce the call types from early in development, suggesting the basic phonetic forms are innately specified. However, the appropriate *contexts* for call use — particularly the nuanced distinction between base and suffixed forms — appear to require social learning. Young males show looser context-specificity that tightens with age and social experience.

## Key Researcher / Discovery Story

Karim Ouattara, Alban Lemasson, and Klaus Zuberbühler's 2009 *PNAS* paper "Campbell's monkeys use affixation to alter call meaning" reported the systematic "-oo" suffix effect, generating substantial debate in animal communication and linguistics.

The finding was significant because it suggested rule-governed combination — not just a fixed set of discrete calls, but productive modification of existing calls by an appended morpheme. Linguists were cautious: the "suffix" interpretation requires that the "-oo" be genuinely compositional (the meaning of the combination is derived from the meanings of the parts) rather than a separate call that happens to sound similar. Subsequent playback studies by Zuberbühler and colleagues supported the compositional interpretation, but the debate over whether this constitutes "syntax" continues.

The cross-species reception is also notable: Diana monkeys (*C. diana*) and other sympatric species in the Taï Forest eavesdrop on Campbell's monkey alarms and respond appropriately — extending the signal's reach beyond the producing species' own social group.

## Open Questions

- **Is the "-oo" modification genuinely compositional?** Or are suffixed forms learned as holistic units that happen to be phonetically similar to base calls? The former would imply a morphological rule; the latter a larger but still fixed repertoire
- **Female call structure**: Most research focuses on male calls; female vocal behavior in this species is comparatively understudied
- **Ontogeny of call combinations**: How do juveniles learn when to produce Hok+Krak sequences vs. individual calls? Is there a critical period?
- **Geographic variation**: Do Campbell's monkey populations in different parts of West Africa show dialectal variation in call forms or usage contexts?

# Humpback Whale Song — Cultural Transmission Across Ocean Basins

Humpback whale song is the most structurally complex animal vocalization outside of human speech, and the clearest demonstration that cultural transmission operates at ocean-basin scale in a non-human species. All males in a geographic region sing essentially the same song at any given time — and that song changes continuously, with innovations spreading across populations without ever revisiting prior forms.

## What the Signal Is

Only males sing (during mating season), in a hierarchical structure of nested units:

- **Unit** — single uninterrupted sound emission, up to a few seconds; frequency-modulated or amplitude-modulated; 20 Hz to 24 kHz
- **Sub-phrase** — 4–6 units, ~10 seconds
- **Phrase** — 2 sub-phrases
- **Theme** — repeated phrase, lasting ~2–4 minutes
- **Song** — collection of themes; up to 30 minutes total; repeated continuously for hours or days

Marine biologist Philip Clapham called it "probably the most complex in the animal kingdom." The "Russian doll" nesting structure — with meaningful units organized into higher-order units across multiple levels — is more human-like in its hierarchical depth than birdsong, which has only linear structure.

## What It Encodes

The honest answer is: unknown. The two candidate functions are:

1. **Male fitness advertisement** — the predominant hypothesis; singing correlates with the mating season and complex song may signal genetic quality or developmental health to females
2. **Male–male competition** — song may coordinate male spacing, reduce aggressive encounters

Neither hypothesis has strong direct evidence. There is no confirmed link between song complexity and reproductive success, and no evidence that females actively select based on song. The function of the feeding call (a separate 5–10 second call used before cooperative lunge-feeding) is also unknown.

The question of whether whales sing for aesthetic enjoyment is considered "untestable" by researchers — this is the honest epistemic boundary.

## Cultural Transmission: The Key Finding

All whales in the same ocean basin sing virtually the same song at any time. Songs from non-overlapping populations are entirely different. Within a population, the song changes slowly and continuously — over months, a particular unit may shift in frequency, a theme may lengthen or drop out. Over 19 years of recording, analysis found that **the same combination of elements never recurred**. Old patterns are abandoned, not revisited.

This is cultural transmission by population consensus, operating at a scale of thousands of individuals across millions of square kilometers of ocean. The mechanism of change propagation is unknown — how does an innovation starting with one whale spread to all whales in a basin while maintaining consensus?

An even more striking finding: songs from adjacent populations with acoustic overlap show directional propagation — a new song pattern can be tracked spreading geographically from a point of origin over months.

## Connection to Sperm Whale Codas

The sperm whale entry in this vault ([[sperm-whale-coda-dialects]] when written) shows a different cetacean cultural transmission system: codas (short rhythmic click sequences of 3–12 clicks) that vary by clan and function as **symbolic group identity markers**. Hersh et al. (2022, *PNAS*) described sperm whale clan codas as "arbitrary traits that function as reliable indicators of cultural group membership" — the strongest claim for symbolic non-human cultural marking yet made. Humpback song and sperm whale codas represent different ends of the cetacean cultural transmission spectrum: one a dynamically evolving broadcast signal, the other a stable identity badge.

## Discovery Story

Frank Watlington, a US Navy hydrophone operator in Bermuda monitoring Soviet submarines, first noticed the sounds and brought them to the Paynes. Katy and Roger Payne published their analysis and released *Songs of the Humpback Whale* (1970) — the best-selling natural history album of its time, which catalyzed the global anti-whaling movement and put whale acoustics on the scientific map.

The wartime sonar infrastructure that detected the sounds is also why the Cold War matters to cetology: whale low-frequency calls (10–40 Hz) travel thousands of kilometers in the SOFAR channel; the same detection capacity used to track submarines was repurposed to study migration. William Schevill (WHOI) even helped defuse Cold War tension when he identified low-frequency signals suspected to be Soviet communications as fin whale calls.

## Open Questions

- What determines the pace of song evolution? Some years change rapidly, others slowly — why?
- How do innovations propagate: direct contact transmission, or some kind of broadcast adoption?
- Does song complexity honestly signal anything about male quality, or is it a runaway process?
- Anthropogenic noise has reduced the functional range of whale communication by orders of magnitude. Blue whale call frequencies have been decreasing by ~0.35 Hz/year since the 1960s — possibly in response to increasing ocean noise. What this means for population-level communication is unknown.
